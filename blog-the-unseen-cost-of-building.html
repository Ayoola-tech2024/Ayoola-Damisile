The Unseen Cost of Building: 14-Hour Days, Expensive Data, and Why We Still Show Up

By Ayoola Damisile · Software engineer and classical pianist · 22 Oct 2026 · 6 min read

There is a polished version of software development that lives on Twitter and LinkedIn. It takes place in a sunlit loft with an ultra-wide monitor, a steaming cup of artisan coffee, and an AI assistant that effortlessly writes a profitable SaaS before lunch. In that world, code is effortless, compute is infinite, and the biggest stress of the day is choosing which framework to debate online.

Then there is the reality most of us live in.

I am writing this not just for myself, but for every developer out there fighting quiet, exhausting battles that nobody ever posts about. The builders who don't have corporate expense cards, who don't have venture capital safety nets, and who have to fight tooth and nail through physical and financial friction just to watch their code compile.

The Literal Cash Price of npm install

Nobody warns you about the financial bleed of simply trying to learn or build when you live outside the fiber-optic bubble. When you rely on mobile data, you quickly learn that software development has a meter running on it every single second.

You run npx create-next-app or install a UI component library, and you literally watch 400MB to 700MB of expensive data vanish into a fresh node_modules folder before you have typed a single line of logic. You exhaust money just buying data packs every day, prioritizing megabytes over personal comfort because if you don't buy data, you can't push commits, you can't read docs, and you can't test API calls. Before you even have a product, before you make a single dollar, just running your local development environment feels like paying a tax for daring to build.

14 Hours on a Hard Chair in a Tech Hub

When the neighborhood power cuts out and your home connection crawls to a halt, you can't afford to sit around waiting for conditions to become ideal. You pack your laptop, your charger, and an extension cord into a backpack like survival gear and head out to a local tech hub.

And then you sit. Not for two hours. You sit for 13 hours, 14 hours, sometimes 15 hours straight on a rigid wooden or plastic chair. You sit until your lower back is numb, your posture is wrecked, and your eyes burn from the relentless glare of the monitor. You stay glued to that seat because that room has a generator that's currently roaring and Wi-Fi that's currently alive. You stay until the hub is closing its doors, milking every single watt of electricity and every kilobyte of bandwidth you can get before you have to step back out into the dark.

"When every resource is scarce, you don't work until you're tired. You work until the generator stops or the data expires."

The "Free Tier" Heart Attack and the 2 AM Ghost

Building on modern serverless and Backend-as-a-Service (BaaS) platforms comes with its own psychological toll. You watch your database dashboards on Supabase or PostgreSQL with constant anxiety, watching the disk usage crawl toward the 500MB free-tier ceiling. You test transactional auth flows and password resets, hyper-aware that an infinite loop could blow through your 100 free emails on Resend or trigger a billing charge on a card you can't afford to burn.

And then comes the 2 AM collapse. You tested the feature at 9 PM. It worked cleanly. You went to grab water, you didn't touch a line of code, and when you return at 2 AM, the deployment fails with a silent error. A third-party webhook timed out, an upstream dependency published a minor patch that broke a sub-dependency, or a serverless function exceeded its execution budget. You sit alone in the pitch black, illuminated only by the cold blue light of a terminal, questioning your sanity and wondering why everything you touch feels so fragile.

Fighting AI on a 50,000-Line Codebase

The tech internet loves to claim that "AI writes all the code now." But that fantasy only works if you're building a 100-line todo app or if you have hundreds of dollars a month to pump into frontier model API tokens. When you're managing a real-world codebase with 50,000+ lines of production code, and you have to rely on smaller, cheaper models because you can't afford massive token burn, the reality is completely different.

The small models don't save you—they hallucinate. When you prompt them to refactor an endpoint or add a component, they lose track of your architecture. They silently wipe out helper functions, invent non-existent parameters, introduce security regressions, and begin churning out confident nonsense. What should have been a 5-minute task turns into three hours of high-stress forensic cleanup, untangling the mess that an AI made while you frantically revert Git commits.

The Unspoken Isolation: "Why Did You Stop Calling?"

Perhaps the hardest tax of all isn't the data bills or the server crashes—it's the detachment from people. Friends and acquaintances stop calling because your answer is always the same: "I'm on my PC, I have to fix something."

People start to assume you're acting proud, antisocial, or that you think you're too good to hang out. They don't understand that when you have to fight for power, fight for data, fight for compute, and fight bugs all at once, your work takes three times longer than it would in an air-conditioned office with gigabit fiber. By the time you shut your laptop lid, your social battery is at absolute zero. You're not being arrogant; you're just exhausted from surviving the friction.

The High That Makes It All Worth It

So why do we do it? Why do we wake up the next morning, recharge the data, pack the bag, and sit right back down on that hard chair?

Because when that feature finally works... something electric happens inside you.

When you fix the bug that haunted you for three days, you don't just feel relieved—you feel on top of the world. When you open Google Search Console in the morning and see impressions and clicks climbing, knowing that real humans from different countries are finding answers on pages you designed, you feel a deep sense of accomplishment. When you publish an open-source tool or an npm package and someone you will never meet stars your repository, forks your code, or runs npx on a tool you engineered on that hard chair, the gratification is unmatched.

It was never just about money. It's the profound knowledge that something you built out of thin air, under the toughest constraints, provided genuine value to another human being somewhere on this planet.

We Can Build a Better Internet

Living through these struggles hasn't made me cynical; it has made me obsessed with resilience. It taught me why zero-dependency devtools like envvault and diagnostic utilities like fixmcp need to be fast, lightweight, and respectful of bandwidth. It taught me why systems must be built to survive network drops, offline edge environments, and real-world chaos.

I believe we can build a better internet—one that doesn't bloat devices, doesn't assume unlimited Wi-Fi, and treats every byte of user bandwidth with respect.

My ambition is to take this grit, this technical endurance, and this refusal to break under pressure into the world's most demanding engineering teams—places like Google, Amazon, and world-class distributed teams building infrastructure that billions of people depend on. Because when you've learned how to build high-performance systems when everything is working against you, building at scale isn't an intimidating challenge; it's the exact arena you've been training for your entire life.

To every developer sitting in a crowded hub right now on hour 13, watching your data pack run low and wondering if anyone sees your effort: keep shipping. The chairs are hard, the bills are invisible, but the craft is yours. We see you.
